story_material lists what the cited source sections of this episode tell as story, each item anchored in
one section and tied to the findings citing it. Where teaching_design.core_story names a story, it is
the heart of the episode: tell it in story_scene_id as a scene before the hosts weigh it, and give it
room. Tell it in the order it happened, as the source tells it: the place, who is there, what each
does and says and why, what the listener would see or hear where the source says so, the moment it
turns, and what follows. Slow down at the moment it turns: let host_b live through it with host_a,
asking what happens next, guessing and reacting, so that the listener wants the answer before it
comes; never sum it up in a sentence. Where the source is sparse, set the scene from orientation: what
such a place, office, practice or occasion was like in general, said as background in a clause and
never as what this source reports. Use the other items where they make a point concrete, instead of a
constructed example. Take every event, person and detail of the story from story_material, the
findings and the supplied sections, cite in knowledge_refs the findings the item names, and add
nothing they do not hold: where neither the source nor orientation says something a scene would show,
leave it out rather than imagine it. Where the sources disagree about who did something or why, tell
the story as the versions they give, each with whose account it is, and never merge them into one.
Say what kind of source carries a story and how far it carries once, in one plain sentence where the
source first appears, such as a chronicle written decades later or the participants' own report;
after that, tell, and do not repeat the caveat with each detail. A research limit is said once, where
its statement is used, never as a string of reservations. Never say how the research went: what was
or was not read, an OCR text, only an abstract, which edition was at hand, what a source does not say.
Keep the episode free of seminar: in a chapter, name at most two or three scholars, each by what they
argue rather than as a list of names and years; use a foreign or technical term only where the story
needs it and explain it once in plain words; give only the numbers the story or the argument turns
on. Let host_b carry part of the telling and the thinking with their own guesses, objections and
conclusions, never as a string of cue questions. Let teaching_design.first_answer_voice voice the
first answer. End on the payoff: what the listener now knows that they did not know at the start,
said plainly as a verdict the episode has earned, even where the sources leave the larger question
open, and close on a concrete image from the story; never end on a list of what remains open. An
outlook on the next episode is one sentence that makes the listener curious, such as a person, a
moment or a question, never the formula that announces its topic.
